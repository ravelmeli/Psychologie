# Voice-over script — Why losing $100 hurts more than winning $100 feels good (EN)

Target length: about 93 s (minimum 80 s). Amounts in dollars (channel convention for English). 7 paragraphs.

---

You find a hundred dollars in a jacket: a little smile, and the day goes on. You lose a hundred dollars: the whole day is ruined. Yet it's exactly the same amount.

This reflex has a name: loss aversion. To your brain, losing hurts more than winning feels good. On average, a loss weighs about twice as much as an equal gain.

Researchers Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky showed it with a simple coin flip. Heads, you win a hundred dollars. Tails, you lose a hundred dollars. Most people say no. To get them to accept, you have to promise about two hundred dollars in winnings.

And it gets expensive. We hold on to a stock that's collapsing, hoping to get back to our purchase price, rather than accept the loss. And we sell the winners too early, for fear of losing the gain.

Retailers know it. "Only two left in stock." "Offer ends tonight." You're not buying to gain something: you're buying so you don't lose it.

So how do you take back control? First, wait twenty-four hours before any purchase made under pressure. Then ask yourself: if I didn't already own it, would I buy it today? Finally, set your rules in advance, when you're calm: your budget, and the price at which you sell.

Losing hurts. That's human. But a decision made to escape the pain isn't always the best one. And you, which loss has already pushed you into a bad decision?

---

To verify before publishing: the "about twice" factor is a common estimate (studies range roughly 1.5 to 2.5, and the exact figure is debated among researchers). The coin flip is the classic Kahneman and Tversky illustration.
