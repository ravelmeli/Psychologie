# Voice-over script — "The $4 coffee isn't what's ruining you" (EN)

Target length: about 90 s (minimum 80 s). Amounts in dollars (channel convention), spelled out for the voice. 9 paragraphs.
(The numeric example is an illustration, not a statistic.)

---

You're told to quit your coffee to get rich. That's wrong.

Let's do the math. A four-dollar coffee, twenty days a month: eighty dollars. On a three-thousand-dollar salary, that's under three percent of your budget. Even if you cut it entirely, almost nothing changes.

Now look at the big categories. Housing, transportation and food often make up more than half of a household's spending. In our example: a thousand dollars of rent, four hundred and fifty for transportation, five hundred for groceries. The rent alone is more than twelve times your coffee budget.

So why is this myth so widespread? Because you see the coffee go by every day. It's visible, easy to count, easy to cut. Insurance, your phone plan, your energy contract: you renew them once a year without thinking. Our brain focuses on what's small and daily, not on what's big.

And this myth has a hidden cost: guilt. You skip a small pleasure, you feel virtuous, and you never touch the real levers.

So where do you act? First, on the big categories, once a year. Compare or renegotiate your insurance, your phone plan, your energy, your bank. One hour of work can beat years without coffee.

Then, automate your savings, so you stop relying on willpower with every purchase.

Finally, keep your pleasures, but choose them. If that coffee truly makes you happy, keep it. If it's an automatic reflex, cut it. Small repeated expenses do add up, but they aren't the priority.

The coffee isn't your problem. The big decisions you make once a year are. And you, which expense category really weighs on you? Tell us in the comments.

---

To verify before publishing:
- The example ($3,000 salary, $4 coffee × 20 days, $1,000 rent, $450 transportation, $500 groceries) is an invented illustration, introduced as such ("In our example").
- "Housing, transportation and food: often more than half of household spending": true as an order of magnitude in the US (BLS) and France (INSEE), but exact shares vary by year and household. The script says "often", not "always".
- $80 / $3,000 = 2.7%: "under three percent". $1,000 / $80 = 12.5: "more than twelve times".
- The advice to compare or renegotiate is general, not personalized financial advice.
