# Voice-over script — Why you keep a subscription you don't use (EN)

Target length: about 90 s (minimum 80 s). Amounts in dollars (channel convention), spelled out for the voice. 9 paragraphs.
(The gym example is an illustration, not a statistic.)

---

You pay forty dollars a month for a gym. You haven't been in six months. And you don't cancel. Why?

Because your brain says: "I've already paid." This trap is called the sunk cost fallacy.

A sunk cost is money, time, or effort already spent that you won't get back, whatever you decide. For economists, a good decision looks only at the future: what's gone is gone.

But the brain hates the idea of losing. Stopping means admitting the money is lost. So we keep going, to feel like we're not wasting it.

In the 1980s, researchers Hal Arkes and Catherine Blumer observed this with theater tickets: people who paid full price attended more performances than people who got a discount. Same play, same enjoyment, but a heavier past expense.

You live it every day. Finishing a meal you don't like, because it's paid for. Watching a boring movie to the end. Keeping a useless subscription. Continuing a project that isn't working. The more you've invested, the harder it is to stop.

So how do you get out? First, ask the right question: if I hadn't paid anything yet, would I do this today? If the answer is no, stop.

Then count the future cost. Each extra month is forty dollars more. The past is gone, but the future you can still save. Every month is a new decision.

Finally, turn the loss into a lesson. What you've already paid is the price of learning it didn't suit you. And you, which subscription are you paying for without using it? Tell us in the comments.

---

To verify before publishing:
- Arkes and Blumer, "The Psychology of Sunk Cost," Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 1985. The theater-ticket study is the classic illustration; the script stays cautious ("observed," no percentage). Verify the exact reference before citing it.
- The gym example ($40 a month, six months without going) is invented for illustration.
- The tips (ask "would I do it today?", count the future cost) are general, not personalized financial advice.
