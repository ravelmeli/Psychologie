# Voice-over script — "Your salary doubled… and you have no more savings" (EN)

Target length: about 88 s (minimum 80 s). Amounts in dollars (channel convention), spelled out for the voice. 9 paragraphs.
(The numeric example is an illustration, not a statistic.)

---

You earn more than you did five years ago. So why has your bank account barely moved?

Take an example. You earn two thousand dollars a month, you spend nineteen hundred, and you save a hundred. Your salary goes up to three thousand dollars. A year later, you spend twenty-nine hundred. And you're still saving a hundred.

This is called lifestyle inflation. Often, every raise becomes the new normal, and spending rises to meet it.

Behind it is a psychological mechanism: hedonic adaptation. Our brain gets used to improvements fast. The bigger apartment, the Friday dinner out, the more comfortable car: after a few months, it's no longer a luxury. It's the minimum. Psychologists call it the hedonic treadmill: you keep running, but you stay in the same place.

And the trap is that each upgrade looks reasonable. A slightly pricier rent. One more subscription. One more trip. No single expense is crazy, but together they swallow the entire raise.

So how do you get out of it? First, look at your savings rate, not your salary. What matters isn't what you earn, it's what you keep.

Then apply a simple rule to every raise: half goes to savings or investing before your lifestyle adjusts. You can enjoy the other half without guilt.

Finally, choose your upgrades. One or two that truly make you happier, not every one your new salary allows.

Earning more doesn't make you rich. Keeping more does. And you, which expense has appeared since your last raise? Tell us in the comments.

---

To verify before publishing:
- The example ($2,000 → $3,000, spending $1,900 → $2,900) is an invented illustration, introduced as such ("Take an example").
- "Hedonic treadmill" comes from Brickman and Campbell (1971). Adaptation is real but partial according to studies: the script says "often", not "always".
- The "half of every raise" rule is an educational suggestion, not personalized financial advice.
