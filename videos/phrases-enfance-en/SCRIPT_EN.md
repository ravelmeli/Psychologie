# Voice-over script — Money phrases heard in childhood (EN)

Target length: about 90 s (minimum 80 s). 9 paragraphs.
No dollar amounts in this script. The example phrases are English equivalents of the French ones.

---

"Money doesn't grow on trees." You've probably heard that sentence as a kid. And it may still be steering your choices today.

Researchers call these money phrases beliefs, or "money scripts." Psychologist Brad Klontz writes about them: ideas learned early, rarely chosen.

Think about what was said at your home. "We can't afford it." "Rich people are dishonest." "We don't talk about money at the table." "Money can't buy happiness." Each of these phrases was repeated dozens of times, and never once discussed.

As an adult, they turn into reflexes. If you grew up hearing "we can't afford it," you avoid looking at your account. If you heard "money doesn't grow on trees," you feel guilty spending, even when you can afford it. If you heard "rich people are dishonest," you distrust success, including your own.

The trap is that you don't see them. They sound like common sense. You never decided to believe them: it's a habit set long before you could judge it.

So how do you free yourself? First, spot the phrase. When you hesitate to spend, to ask for a raise, or to look at your account, ask yourself: what sentence do I hear in my head?

Then find where it comes from. Who said it, and in what situation? Often, it was protecting someone from a specific reality that may no longer be yours.

Finally, rewrite it. Not to deny it, but to make it true today. "Money doesn't grow on trees" can become: "Money gets built, and I'm allowed to enjoy it."

And you, which money phrase did you hear at home? Write it in the comments.

---

To verify before publishing:
- "Money scripts" is a concept from psychologist Brad Klontz and colleagues (2010s). He describes four main types (avoidance, worship, status, vigilance). The script doesn't detail them and only says "ideas learned early, rarely chosen": verify the exact wording before citing a source.
- The four example phrases are common examples, not statistics. The behaviors described (avoiding your account, guilt about spending, distrusting success) are plausible illustrations, not measured results.
- Spotting and rewriting a belief is personal reflection, not therapy. The disclaimer goes in the description.
