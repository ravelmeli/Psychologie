# Voice-over script — Why $9.99 makes you buy (EN)

Target length: about 85 s (minimum 80 s). Amounts in dollars (channel convention), spelled out for the voice. 9 paragraphs.
Suggested pause: 2 seconds of silence after paragraph 1 (time to choose).

---

Which price looks lower: four dollars, or three ninety-nine? Obviously, three ninety-nine. But look at the gap: a single cent.

One cent. Yet it's enough to change how your brain reads the price. This is called the left-digit effect.

We read prices from left to right, and the first digit weighs too much. Faced with three ninety-nine, the brain remembers "three dollars and change." Faced with four dollars, it remembers "four dollars." It feels a gap far bigger than that one cent.

Researchers, including Manoj Thomas and Vicki Morwitz, have studied this bias: when the left digit drops a notch, people underestimate the real difference.

Retailers know it. Nine ninety-nine. Nineteen ninety-nine. Forty-nine ninety-nine. A price just under the threshold, from coffee to couches.

And the trap goes further: we file the product under the left digit. "Under ten dollars." "Under fifty dollars." We no longer compare to our budget, we compare to a threshold.

So how do you defend yourself? First, always round up in your head. Nine ninety-nine is ten dollars.

Then ask yourself: would I buy it at ten dollars? If the answer is no, it's not the product that attracts you, it's the number.

Finally, look at the price per ounce or per unit, not the displayed price, and at what the purchase represents in your month.

A price isn't just a number: it's a message to your brain. And you, which price ending in nine has already made you buy? Tell us in the comments.
