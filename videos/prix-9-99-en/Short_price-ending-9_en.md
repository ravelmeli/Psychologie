## Description

Which price looks lower: four dollars, or $3.99? Obviously $3.99. But the real gap is just one cent.

That one cent is enough to change how your brain reads the price: this is the left-digit effect. We read prices from left to right, and the first digit weighs too much. Faced with $3.99, the brain remembers "three dollars and change"; faced with $4.00, "four dollars." It feels a gap far bigger than that one cent. Researchers, including Manoj Thomas and Vicki Morwitz, have studied this bias: when the left digit drops a notch, people underestimate the real difference.

Retailers know it: $9.99, $19.99, $49.99, a price just under the threshold, from coffee to couches. We then file the product under the left digit ("under $10," "under $50") and compare to a threshold instead of our budget.

How to defend yourself: 1. always round up in your head ($9.99 is $10); 2. ask yourself "would I buy it at $10?" (if not, it's the number attracting you, not the product); 3. look at the price per ounce or per unit, and at what the purchase represents in your month.
